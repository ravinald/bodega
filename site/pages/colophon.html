Colophon

A bodega, in New York, is a corner store: by the city Health Department's definition, a shop of under 300 square meters that sells milk, meat or eggs, is not a specialty store, and has no more than two cash registers.6 The word is Spanish, from the Latin apotheca and the Greek apothēkē, a depot or store,1 the same word that gave French its boutique; English took it in 1846, as a wine shop.2

In Spanish the word names the places things are put away: a cellar where wine is kept and aged, a granary, a warehouse in a seaport, the hold of a ship.1 Mexican Spanish kept that sense. El Colegio de México's dictionary gives only two meanings, a covered place where something is stored (bodega de granos, bodega de vinos) and the part of a shop or house where goods are kept,3 and the Spanish-language academies list the warehouse sense first for Mexico.4

New York heard the word first as a Spanish barroom, which The Sun reported in 1902 the city would soon have.5 The grocery came with the Puerto Ricans who arrived from the 1920s: the first Puerto Rican bodegas opened on Franklin Avenue in Greenpoint,5 and by the late 1920s more than 200 bodegas and colmados served between 150,000 and 200,000 Puerto Ricans, spreading out from East Harlem and Gowanus.6 They were where a newcomer heard about work or housing, and bought on credit when money ran short.6 After the war the community grew tenfold, from about 60,000 in 1940 to more than 600,000 in 1960.7 The Cuban photographer Justo Martí took nearly 200 photographs of Puerto Rican-owned bodegas for the Puerto Rican Merchant Association, to satisfy the state's liquor license application; the collection has been at the Center for Puerto Rican Studies at Hunter College since 1984.8

Dominican immigrants bought many of those stores from their Puerto Rican and Cuban owners, and by 1991 owned about 80 percent of the city's roughly 9,000 Latino bodegas and independent groceries.9 Yemeni Americans followed; their merchants' association estimated in 2022 that they owned about half of the city's bodegas.5 Nobody agrees on how many there are. The Bodega Association of the United States estimated 13,000 in 2017,7 and the Health Department, counting by its own definition, about 7,000.6 The cat asleep on the counter is not legal in any of them: a city council bill to allow it was introduced in November 2025.10

The software takes its name from both senses of the word. Its author lived in New York, where a bodega is the shop on the corner; the author's father is Mexican, and in Mexico a bodega is where goods are kept.
